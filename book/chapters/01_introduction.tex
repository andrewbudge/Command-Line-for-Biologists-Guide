The impact that computers have had on humanity cannot be overstated. Since the invention of the modern electronic computer in the year 1946, the use of them has been incorporated into nearly every aspect of life. You interact with computers on a daily basis. What began as simple machines for arithmetic have evolved into machines more powerful than ever imagined. The phone you hold in your hand is roughly 100{,}000 times more powerful than the guidance computer aboard Apollo 11, the spacecraft that first put men on the moon on July 20, 1969.

In addition to the incredible development and improvement of computers, biological data is being produced in unprecedented amounts. In 1977, Frederick Sanger and colleagues sequenced the 5{,}375~bp genome of the bacteriophage $\phi$X174. Now, almost 50 years later, there are over 4.7 billion sequences available in public, online databases.

\section{The Cost of Convenience}

Computers are powerful, but that power is often unrecognized and unused. When computers were first developed, they were almost exclusively scientific and industrial tools. They were confined to universities and research labs, running massive calculations on hardware that filled entire rooms in some cases. Today, computers fill another role entirely: communication and entertainment. Almost everyone carries a personal computer in their pocket. A file created in one corner of the world can be shared with thousands of people in seconds.

The way we \emph{interact} with computers has shifted just as dramatically. Early computers were operated entirely through text---no mouse, no icons, no windows. Using one took serious training. As computers shifted toward everyday consumer use, the interfaces adapted: windows, folders, drag-and-drop, the graphical interface we now take for granted. These interfaces are easy to learn, quick to use, and have made the power of computers more accessible than ever before.

But accessibility has come at a cost. Each layer of polish on top of the machine hides another layer of what's underneath. Most people know how to open a word processor; far fewer know---or need to know---what the program is actually doing on their behalf. That's fine for everyday use. The problem isn't that ordinary users don't know how their computer works; the problem is that as scientists, \emph{we} are now everyday users too, and the tools we've inherited are built for clicking and dragging, not for the scale and reproducibility that science demands.

Working underneath the graphical interface---at the command line---is a partly lost art, and one this book is trying to bring back. It is not glamorous. It is harder to learn than clicking buttons. But it pays for itself the first time you rename five hundred files in a single command, chain three tools into a pipeline that runs in seconds, or hand a colleague a script that reproduces your entire analysis from raw data. The command line trades accessibility for power, modularity, and reproducibility---the three things a biologist working with modern data needs most.

\section{Computers as Scientific Instruments}

The first step in unlocking the power of computation, is in how you view computers. Most view computers as a black box, a magic machine with a screen that does what we click on it to do. Most find computers useful, but often frustrating at the same time, as they do not do what we want them to do at times. Shifting our view of computers from something we hope will do what we want to, to a tool, is fundamental. Computers will only do exactly what we tell them to do, not what we want them to do.
